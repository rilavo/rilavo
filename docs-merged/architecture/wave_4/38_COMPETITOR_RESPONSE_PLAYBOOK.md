# Rilavo Product — Competitor Response Playbook (E-38)

**Tree:** Product
**Wave:** 4 — Survival & Defensibility
**Status:** Decided (principle and trigger-mapping); depends on E-25's archetypes for full context
**Depends on:** E-25
**Closes when:** Tested at the first real competitive threat — a playbook that's never been exercised is a draft, not a proven procedure, same caveat as P-12's incident runbook.

## The principle

Don't price-war. A competitor with more capital can always underprice Rilavo further; competing on that axis is a fight against infrastructure economics, decided in every founding document as unwinnable and therefore not worth entering.

## Trigger-to-response mapping, tied to E-25's competitor archetypes

| Trigger | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| A hyperscaler bundles an equivalent feature into an existing platform, free | Accelerate standards-body engagement and cross-platform integration density — a bundled feature doesn't help a customer who needs interoperability *across* platforms | Cut prices to match "free" — cannot be matched, and trying signals panic, not confidence |
| A well-funded, dedicated startup competitor launches | Accelerate the assets that don't fork — accumulated fraud intelligence, uptime track record, standards legitimacy | React publicly or urgently — a new entrant with zero installed verifiers is not yet a threat to respond to, only to watch |
| A community fork appears with no company behind it | Assess whether it strengthens or fragments the open standard, per Protocol Principle 5 | Treat it as automatically hostile — a fork that genuinely improves the shared protocol is a win for the ecosystem even if it's not a Rilavo Product win specifically |
| A competitor claims better fraud detection or uptime | Publish real, verifiable operating history in response — the actual evidence a well-tuned trust graph is supposed to produce | Make unverifiable counter-claims — this is exactly the kind of claim E-26's moat scorecard exists to make checkable instead of asserted |

## Worked example — the fork scenario, walked through

A well-resourced entity forks the open protocol's reference implementation and launches a competing hosted service, undercutting on price. Per the table above: no price match. Instead, Rilavo doubles down on what a fork starts with zero of — its own installed verifier base, its accumulating fraud-pattern intelligence, and its standing with whatever standards bodies it has engaged (P-18, P-24). This is the practical expression of E-25 and E-26's reasoning, turned into an actual sequence of moves rather than left as analysis.

## What this playbook cannot do

Guarantee the response works. A playbook is a plan for how to compete, not a proof that competing succeeds — that proof, if it exists, comes from E-41 and E-42's monitoring frameworks actually showing retention and growth holding up under real competitive pressure, not from this document alone.

## What would change this decision

The first real competitive threat, of any archetype — which either validates this playbook's response choices or reveals a trigger this table didn't anticipate, in which case the table gets a new row, not a rewritten principle.
